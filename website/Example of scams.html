A WARNING ON YOUR SCREEN IS NOT AN EMERGENCY.

Stop. Don't call the number. Don't click the button. Don't give anyone remote access.

A webpage can make an alarming message look very real. It can display government logos, play an alarm, claim your computer has been infected, tell you that your bank account is in danger, or threaten you with arrest.

Don't panic. These are common scare tactics used by scammers to make you act before you have time to think.

Recognizing the Warning Signs

Scammers are constantly changing their stories, but many of the techniques they use remain the same. They create a problem, make the problem sound urgent, and then tell you that you must do something immediately to make it go away.

The examples below are some of the most common warning signs to watch for.

🚨 Fake FBI, IRS and Government Warnings

One particularly frightening scam involves a webpage that claims to be from the FBI, IRS, Department of Justice, or another government agency.

You may suddenly see a warning claiming that your computer has been involved in illegal activity. The page may display an official-looking government seal, sound an alarm, and provide a telephone number that you are told to call immediately.

It may say things such as:

FBI WARNING: Your computer has been locked due to illegal activity. Call the number below immediately to avoid arrest.

Other versions may claim that you owe money to the IRS, that an arrest warrant has been issued, or that your Social Security number has been suspended.

The webpage may also make it difficult to close the window or navigate away. It may repeatedly display messages or play loud audio to make the situation feel like an emergency.

RED FLAG: A government agency is not going to lock your computer through a web browser and demand that you call a telephone number displayed on the screen.

💻 Fake Windows and Microsoft Virus Alerts

Another common scam is a webpage designed to look like a Windows or Microsoft security warning.

The page may claim that viruses have been detected and instruct you to call "Microsoft Support" immediately.

It may include:

  • A Microsoft or Windows logo
  • A fake virus scan
  • Flashing warnings
  • Loud alarms or computer-generated voices
  • A countdown timer
  • A telephone number to call for "support"
  • Warnings that your computer or files will be damaged

One of the most important things to understand is that a webpage can pretend to scan your computer.

You might see a message saying:

Scanning your computer for viruses...
Threats detected: 17

That does not mean your computer was actually scanned. The animation may simply be part of the webpage designed to frighten you.

RED FLAG: A real security warning should not tell you to call a telephone number displayed in a browser pop-up. The FTC specifically warns that legitimate security pop-ups do not ask you to call a phone number.

🦠 Fake Antivirus and Scareware

Some scams go a step further and try to convince you to install antivirus or security software that you never intended to install.

The software may immediately report dozens or hundreds of "problems" and repeatedly display frightening messages.

127 threats detected!
Your computer is at risk. Remove threats now.

Some questionable security products may:

  • Appear after clicking an advertisement
  • Claim to have found an unusually large number of threats
  • Constantly display urgent warnings
  • Demand payment to remove supposed threats
  • Use names or logos that resemble legitimate security products
  • Claim that your existing antivirus program is defective
  • Install additional software or browser extensions
  • Make themselves difficult to uninstall

This does not mean that every unfamiliar antivirus program is fraudulent. There are many legitimate security products that you may simply not recognize.

The important question is: How did it get there?

RED FLAG: If security software suddenly appears after clicking an alarming advertisement or pop-up, don't assume it is legitimate simply because it says "Antivirus" or displays a familiar-looking logo.

🌐 How to Spot a Fake or Questionable Website

A website can look almost identical to a legitimate website and still be fraudulent. Logos, colors, photographs and professional designs are easy for scammers to copy.

Look carefully at the web address

Don't assume that a website is legitimate simply because it has a familiar company name somewhere in the address.

Be cautious of addresses containing:

  • Unexpected words added to a company name
  • Misspellings
  • Unusual domains
  • Long or confusing addresses
  • Addresses that don't match the organization you intended to visit

Also remember that HTTPS does not prove that a website is legitimate. HTTPS encrypts the connection between your browser and the website. It does not tell you who operates the website.

Be careful with search results and advertisements

A scammer may create an advertisement that appears when someone searches for technical support, antivirus software, banking information, or another popular service.

When possible, go directly to the company's known website rather than calling a telephone number or clicking an unfamiliar advertisement.

RED FLAG: Don't judge a website by its logo or appearance alone. Check the address and consider how you arrived there.

📞 Fake Tech Support

A common scam follows a simple pattern:

Fake warning → Phone number → Remote access → Fake diagnosis → Payment

The scammer may claim to be from Microsoft, Apple, your Internet provider, or another well-known company.

They may ask you to install remote-access software so they can "fix" your computer. Once they have access, they can potentially see files, passwords, financial information, and other information on the computer.

RED FLAG: If someone unexpectedly contacts you and tells you there is a problem with your computer, don't give them remote access.

🏦 Banking and Credit Card Scams

This category can be particularly convincing because banks and credit card companies really do contact customers about suspected fraud.

A legitimate interaction might sound like:

"We noticed a $1,247 purchase on your card. Did you make this purchase?"

If you're unsure who is calling, you can always hang up and call the number on the back of your card.

A suspicious request might instead sound like:

"Your account has been compromised. Give me your complete credit card number, security code, Social Security number and online banking password so I can secure the account."

Don't provide sensitive information simply because someone contacted you and created a sense of urgency.

RED FLAG: If you are uncomfortable with a caller's request, hang up and contact the bank or credit card company using a phone number you obtained independently.

👮 Law Enforcement and Government Impersonation

Scammers may pretend to be police officers, sheriff's deputies, federal agents, IRS employees, or other government officials.

They may claim:

  • There is a warrant for your arrest
  • You missed jury duty
  • Your identity was involved in a crime
  • You owe a large fine
  • Your Social Security number has been compromised
  • Your account will be suspended

The scammer then offers a way to make the problem disappear if you pay immediately or provide sensitive information.

RED FLAG: Fear and urgency are being used to prevent you from stopping and verifying the story.

🤖 AI Voice Cloning and Deepfake Phone Calls

Artificial intelligence has created another problem: a scammer may be able to make a phone call using a voice that sounds very much like someone you know.

Imagine receiving a call that sounds exactly like your child, grandchild, friend, or another family member:

"It's me. I'm in trouble. Please don't tell anyone. I need money right now."

Hearing a familiar voice is no longer enough to prove who is on the other end of the phone.

If something sounds unusual, don't rely on the voice alone. Contact the person using a telephone number or another method of communication that you already know is legitimate.

RED FLAG: A caller who insists that you must keep the situation secret, must not hang up, or must send money immediately is giving you another reason to stop and verify.

The Common Pattern Behind Many Scams

The story changes, but the tactics are often remarkably similar.

  • Something is wrong.
    Your account has been compromised. Your computer has a virus. You missed jury duty. You owe money. A family member is in trouble.
  • You must act immediately.
    Don't hang up. Don't tell anyone. Don't call the bank yourself. Don't contact the police.
  • Something terrible will happen if you don't.
    Your account will be suspended. You'll be fined. You'll be arrested. You'll lose your money or your computer files.
  • Then comes the request.
    Give us your password. Give us your Social Security number. Give us your banking information. Give us the verification code. Give us remote access. Send money.

When you see this combination of fear, urgency and a demand for information or money, stop.

If Something Suddenly Scares You

STOP.

  • Don't call the number on the screen.
  • Don't click the button.
  • Don't provide personal information.
  • Don't provide banking or credit card information.
  • Don't give anyone remote access to your computer.
  • Don't send money.
  • Don't let someone else's urgency become your emergency.

If you believe there really may be a problem, contact the company or organization yourself using contact information you obtained independently. Don't use the telephone number, link, or contact information provided by the person or pop-up that frightened you.

If you're not sure what to do, ask someone you trust before clicking, calling, paying, or providing information.

When in Doubt, Ask Before You Click

There is no shame in asking for help. A five-minute conversation with someone you trust can be much easier than recovering from identity theft, financial loss, or a compromised computer.

If something doesn't feel right, stop. You don't have to figure it out by yourself.
